## 5. Evaluating Viral Health Advice – “Can I Trust This Viral Health Advice? How to Know if a Social Media Trend is Good or Bad”  
**Source:** *CU Anschutz Medical Campus – Department of Medicine* – published December 19 2024  
**Link:** https://news.cuanschutz.edu/department-of-medicine/viral-health-advice-social-media-trends  

### Six practical tips for vetting health trends on social media

| # | Tip | Quick check |
|---|-----|-------------|
| 1 | **Identify the creator’s credentials** – look for MD, PhD, RN, or recognized specialist titles. | If no credentials, treat as anecdotal. |
| 2 | **Search for peer‑reviewed evidence** – type the claim into Google Scholar; at least one recent (≤ 5 years) study should support it. | Absence of scholarly support = red flag. |
| 3 | **Check for conflicts of interest** – is the creator selling a product or affiliate link? | Financial motive often biases advice. |
| 4 | **Look for consensus** – reputable health organisations (CDC, WHO, NHS) should echo the claim. | Consensus across ≥ 2 major bodies = higher reliability. |
| 5 | **Assess the plausibility of the mechanism** – does the claim align with known physiology? | Implausible mechanisms (e.g., “detoxifying” via a single herb) are usually false. |
| 6 | **Test the “reversal” test** – if you stop the trend, does anything change? (e.g., no withdrawal symptoms). | Sudden dependence suggests a marketing hook. |

*The article also provides a printable “Health‑Trend Vetting Checklist” and links to a curated list of trustworthy health‑information sites.*
